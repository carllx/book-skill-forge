# Workflow: Managing Size, Space, and Composition

## Prerequisites & Context
- **Why and When**: This workflow governs the manipulation of scale, spatial illusion, and composition. Apply these guidelines when making decisions about aspect ratios, establishing focal points, creating atmospheric depth, and utilizing colour temperature to guide the viewer's eye. 
- **Deep Dives**: For deep theoretical background on specific historical examples (such as Velazquez's spatial mastery in *Las Meninas*, Bosch's layering, or Renaissance perspective), strictly query the `graphify_query` MCP tool.

## Comprehensive Guide & Best Practices

### 1. Scaling and the Picture Plane
- **Format and Aspect Ratio**: 
  - Use the traditional "portrait format" (taller than wide) to enclose subjects neatly, or an oval shape to suit human proportions. 
  - Ensure the bounding frame provides "breathing space" without chopping off the sitter.
- **Psychology of Scale**:
  - **Huge/Giant Scale**: Creates immediate impact and suggests the subject is divine, noble, or highly prominent (e.g., massive 11m high stone statues).
  - **Miniature Scale**: Fosters personal intimacy, often used for lovetokens or private observation.
- **The Picture Plane Illusion**: Treat the canvas as a "pane of glass." Choose between accentuating its flatness through decorative design, or piercing it to offer a three-dimensional window into another world.

### 2. Creating Depth (Opening Out)
- **The Three Zones Rule**: Compose scenes in three distinct zones to give depth:
  - *Foreground (1)*: Where most of the action takes place; sharply focused.
  - *Middle ground (2)*: The transitional space.
  - *Background (3)*: Distant views, increasingly hazy.
- **Visual Stepping Stones**: Do not let the viewer's eye wander aimlessly. Dot points of interest—such as paths, rivers, figures, or animals—strategically to route the spectator from the foreground to the main focal point.
- **Open vs. Closed Boundaries**: 
  - Use "open-sided" framing to suggest a vast space beyond the confines of the canvas. Let elements bleed off the edge to widen the pictorial scope and engage the imagination.

> **Show, Don't Tell: Visual Stepping Stones**
> Highlight natural elements like water to allow the eye to roam back and forth across the picture, leading directly to the focal point (e.g., a central cathedral).

### 3. Aerial Perspective and Distance
- **Atmospheric Mechanics**: Mimic the natural effect of light scattering. Make distant objects progressively paler and bluer, as shorter blue wavelengths travel through the atmosphere more easily.
- **Focus Shifts**: Keep foreground objects in sharp focus and make background objects hazy.
- **Classic Renaissance Zoning**: Use green for the foreground, brown for the middle ground, and blue for the background to mathematically partition space.

> **Show, Don't Tell: Hazy Hills**
> Mountain ranges should become distinctly blue in the middle distance and progressively paler towards the background.

### 4. Colour Temperature and Contrast
- **Push and Pull Mechanics**:
  - *Warm Colours* (red, orange, yellow): Appear to advance toward the viewer. Use them for immediate foreground elements or focal subjects.
  - *Cool Colours* (blue, green, violet): Appear to recede into the distance. Use them for backgrounds and shadows.
- **Complementary Vibrations**: Pair opposite colours (yellow/purple, blue/orange, red/green) side by side. They clash visually, making each other look brighter and establishing separation between overlapping elements.

> **Show, Don't Tell: Pushing with Cool, Pulling with Warm**
> Make foreground subjects stand out instantly by using warm reds and oranges. Accentuate their distance from the background by rendering the backdrop and cloth in cool, receding bluish tones.

## If/Then Troubleshooting Logic

- **IF** the image feels entirely flat or lacks scale, **THEN** divide the composition into foreground, middle ground, and background zones, applying aerial perspective (blur and blue tints) to the deepest layers.
- **IF** the spectator's eye is lost or ignores the main subject, **THEN** introduce visual stepping stones (paths, rivers, contrasting highlights) that point directly to the focal point.
- **IF** foreground elements are blending into the background, **THEN** increase the colour temperature contrast. Paint the foreground subjects with warm, advancing tones and shift the background to cooler, receding tones.
- **IF** the painting feels claustrophobic, **THEN** widen the pictorial scope by employing open-sided framing, letting background landscapes extend off-canvas.

## Verification Checklists

- [ ] **Canvas Impact**: Is the size of the canvas chosen intentionally to create impact (large/imposing) or intimacy (small)?
- [ ] **Formatting**: Does the portrait/landscape format provide sufficient "breathing space" around the subject?
- [ ] **Zonal Separation**: Are the three picture zones (foreground, middle ground, background) distinctly rendered?
- [ ] **Aerial Accuracy**: Do distant elements lose sharpness, shift toward blue/grey, and become paler?
- [ ] **Stepping Stones**: Are there deliberate compositional routes leading the eye to the primary focal point?
- [ ] **Temperature Mapping**: Are advancing elements warm-toned, and receding elements cool-toned?
