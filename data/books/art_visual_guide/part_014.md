# Earth colours

Warm, natural siennas, umbers, and ochres, which make dull reds and mellow yellows, were the staple pigments ofthe Renaissance and Baroque palettes. Earth colours were stable inoil paintand cheap. They couldeither be used raw or roasted like coffee to create a richer colour Some artists, including Rembrandt,primed thepanel or canvas with an earth colour mixture that glowed through the subsequent layers ofpaint. Anycool colours used contrasted with the overall warmth of the painting.

# Technological advances

At the end of the 18th century, a wider range of stable pigments was discovered andby the mid-19th centurya hugenumberof strong new colours had emerged. Packaging methods also improved, making paints portable and farmore easy to use. These advances transformed the ways in which artists worked and also opened up painting to the amateur artist.

# 19th century

Before the 19th centuryartistsd limited range of pigments at their disposal. Towards 1800, chromium was discovered and chrome yellow, viridian (chrome green), and cadmium yellow, orange,and redbecameavailable to buy.Naplesyellow replaced lead-tinyellow. In the19th century, a wholeraft ofartificial dyes produced mauveand more stable blues and greens,such as emerald green (later found tobe toxic) and cobalt blue. These pigments were strong in colour, cheap, and synthetic,and they worked in every medium from oils towatercolour. Even ultramarine was now made synthetically, and called French ultramarine.
